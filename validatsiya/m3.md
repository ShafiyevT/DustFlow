# Computational Experiment

## Study Area and Input Data

The computations were performed for the Bukhara region of Uzbekistan and the adjacent part of the Kyzylkum desert, 61.5°–66.0° E, 38.5°–42.0° N (Fig. 5). About 72% of the area is bare or sparsely vegetated desert, 15% steppe grassland and 9% irrigated cropland of the Zarafshan oasis and the Amu Darya valley. Hourly 10-m wind, 2-m temperature and volumetric soil moisture of the 0–7 cm layer were taken from the ERA5-Land reanalysis (0.1°) [@munoz2021]. Clay content and bulk density of the 0–5 cm layer were taken from SoilGrids 2.0 (250 m) [@poggio2021], terrain from the Copernicus GLO-90 digital elevation model [@copdem], and land cover from ESA WorldCover 2021 (10 m) [@zanaga2022]. Land-cover classes were converted into *z*~0~, λ and *A*~ef~ (bare or sparse vegetation: *z*~0~ = 10^−3^ m, λ = 0.004, *A*~ef~ = 0.25; grassland and shrubland: *z*~0~ = 0.03–0.05 m, λ = 0.03–0.05, *A*~ef~ = 0.10; built-up areas, trees and water: *A*~ef~ = 0). Volumetric moisture θ was converted into gravimetric moisture by *w* = 100 θρ~*w*~/ρ~*b*~ with the SoilGrids bulk density ρ~*b*~.

Two data-quality issues required decisions. First, SoilGrids assigns about 30% clay to all land-cover classes of the domain, including the sandy desert, with a 90% prediction interval of 2%–80%, i.e. it carries practically no information there; since the clay fraction is one of the most influential parameters (Section 8.6), literature values for Kyzylkum sandy soils were used for bare surfaces (*m*~*cl*~ = 0.05) and grassland and shrubland (0.08), and SoilGrids values were retained elsewhere. Second, ERA5-Land does not represent irrigation, so that its soil moisture over the oases is too low; irrigated cropland was therefore excluded from the erodible surface during the growing season (*A*~ef~ = 0). Both decisions were made on physical grounds before the model results were compared with observations.

![Figure 5: Static input fields on the 0.01° intermediate grid: (A) terrain elevation; (B) clay fraction; (C) erodible fraction *A*~ef~; (D) aerodynamic roughness length. Sources: Copernicus GLO-90, SoilGrids 2.0, ESA WorldCover 2021](fig/Fig5.png)

## Computational Setup

The dust storm of 28–29 April 2025 was selected. It was the strongest event over Bukhara in the CAMS reanalysis for March–May 2025 and is documented by the national hydrometeorological service. The storm was driven by northerly winds of 10–13 m/s. The computational parameters are listed in Table 2. The domain of 380 km × 390 km × 5 km was covered by a 60 × 60 × 20 grid (Δ*x* = 6.3 km, Δ*y* = 6.5 km) with a time step of 120 s. The simulation covered 48 h (28 April 00:00 – 30 April 00:00 UTC) with hourly updates of the meteorological forcing. The background concentration at the lateral boundaries was set to zero, so that only emission within the domain was represented. The computation took 228 s on a single processor core (4.7 s per model hour). The sensitivity of the results to the grid spacing and time step is examined in Appendix A.

The emission and near-surface concentration fields at 29 April, 12:00 UTC are shown in Fig. 6. At this hour *u*~*~ ranges from 0.26 to 1.58 m/s over the domain, and the effective threshold from 0.24 to 2.4 m/s. ## Mass Budget and Model Checks

The budget (19) was evaluated at every time step (Fig. 7). Of the emitted mass, 62.1% left the domain through its boundaries, 37.2% was deposited and 0.6% was removed by scavenging, while 2.2% remained airborne at the end. The relative residual of the budget stayed below 0.01% throughout and was 0.0029% at the end. The zeroing of negative undershoots (Section 7) added 20.7 kt, i.e. 2.1% of the emitted mass. This term is accounted for exactly in the budget, and its reduction is one of the directions for improving the scheme. Because the forcing varies in time, the dust mass in the domain does not reach a steady state: it grows with the storm, peaks at 78.7 kt on 29 April at 13:00 UTC and decreases as the wind weakens.

## Fraction-Resolved vs. Single-Mode Transport

This is the main result of the experiment. The same 48-h simulation was repeated with the five transport fractions replaced by a single effective mode that carries the whole emitted mass (1000.8 kt, identical in all runs). The density of the mode was set to the mass-weighted mean density of the fractions, 2250 kg/m^3^. Because the choice of the effective diameter is itself a modelling decision, several values were tested. Two a priori choices were considered: 4.5 µm, the geometric mean of the 0.2–100 µm range, and 10 µm, the PM~10~ cut-off. They were complemented by the mass-median diameter of the emitted distribution (14), 25.5 µm, and by diameters around the value at which the single mode reproduces the dry deposition of the fraction-resolved run (Table 4, Fig. 8).

Note: The differences refer to the 48-h mean near-surface concentration and are evaluated over the 90% of the domain with the highest concentrations.

With the a priori diameters the single mode underestimates dry deposition by a factor of 3.6–4.9 and, consequently, overestimates both the export from the domain and the near-surface concentration: the median difference is about +20%, and the difference exceeds 25% over more than one third of the domain (Fig. 8C). As the diameter increases, the deposition grows monotonically (Fig. 8D). At 29.9 µm it matches the fraction-resolved value, and the bulk budget and the 48-h mean total concentration are then reproduced to within 7% in the median and 9% at the 90th percentile.

A single mode with a tuned diameter can therefore represent the bulk mass budget of this event, and the advantage of size resolution must be sought elsewhere. It lies in two points. First, the matching diameter is not known a priori. It is far from both conventional choices and can only be determined from a size-resolved solution or from deposition measurements, which are rarely available. Nothing guarantees that the same value holds for other wind and mixing conditions, because the mass-weighted settling of a polydisperse aerosol depends on the transport distance. Second, and more importantly for applications, a single mode provides no information on the size composition of the dust. In the fraction-resolved run the contributions of the fractions to the near-surface concentration at the storm peak (29 April, 12:00 UTC) are 6%, 40%, 11%, 34% and 10%, respectively. PM~10~ thus accounts for 46% of the near-surface dust mass, and PM~2.5~ for 6%. Interpreting the total concentration of a single mode as PM~10~, the quantity regulated by air-quality standards, would overestimate PM~10~ by a factor of about two. The PM~2.5~/PM~10~ ratio, which is tested against observations in Section 8.7, cannot be obtained from a single-mode model at all.

## Sensitivity to the Controlling Parameters

Each parameter was varied by ±25% from its base value with the others fixed, for the forcing of 29 April 2025, 09:00 UTC held constant over 6 h on a reduced grid. The sensitivity index was defined as

$$S_p=\frac{\max\left(|\Delta \bar C^{+}|,\,|\Delta \bar C^{-}|\right)/\bar C}{|\Delta p|/p}, \qquad (21)$$

where $\bar C$ is the domain-mean near-surface concentration (Table 5, Fig. 9).

:::

| Parameter | −25% | +25% | *S*~*p*~ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wind speed *U*~10~ | −76.4% | +150.0% | 6.00 |
| Clay fraction *m*~*cl*~ | −66.8% | +109.9% | 4.40 |
| Soil moisture *w* | +29.0% | −29.0% | 1.16 |
| Erodible fraction *A*~ef~ | −25.0% | +25.0% | 1.00 |
| Roughness length *z*~0~ | −22.0% | +20.7% | 0.88 |
| Roughness density λ | +11.6% | −11.7% | 0.47 |
| Terrain elevation *h* | +0.1% | +0.3% | 0.01 |

![Figure 9: Change of the domain-mean near-surface concentration for ±25% variation of each parameter](fig/Fig9.png)

The wind speed is the dominant factor: a 25% increase raises the concentration by a factor of 2.5, owing to the cubic dependence (12) combined with the threshold nature of the emission. Near the threshold the forecast therefore inherits the error of the wind field with strong amplification, and the requirements on the accuracy of meteorological input are especially strict there. The clay fraction is the second factor (*S*~*p*~ = 4.4), because the sandblasting efficiency (13) depends on it exponentially; this quantifies the need for field data on soil texture, which the global soil database does not provide for the Kyzylkum. For the 48-h storm, varying the clay fraction of bare desert surfaces between 0.03 and 0.10 changes the total emission by a factor of 0.91–2.06 relative to the base value of 0.05; the dependence is strongly nonlinear because both the sandblasting efficiency and the residual moisture in Eq. (9) depend on clay content. Soil moisture and roughness density act in the opposite direction, raising the threshold. The index for *A*~ef~ equals unity exactly, as expected from the linear dependence (13), which serves as a control of the implementation. The effect of terrain is negligible because the region is flat.

## Comparison with Observations

Hourly PM~10~ and PM~2.5~ concentrations measured by the automatic monitoring stations of the Hydrometeorological Service of Uzbekistan in Bukhara, Navoi and Karshi were used, together with the CAMS EAC4 reanalysis [@inness2019] as an independent model reference. Because the stations measure the total particulate concentration, including traffic, construction and industrial contributions, whereas the model computes wind-erosion dust only, the model was compared with the excess over background. The background was taken as the median of 20–26 April for the stations and, separately, for CAMS. For comparability, all three data sets were sampled at the 3-hourly CAMS times of 28–30 April. The model run for this comparison used the same configuration with a 24-h spin-up (from 27 April 00:00 UTC).

The results are summarized in Table 6 and Fig. 10. Besides the local-emission configuration used throughout Section 8, a nested configuration was evaluated in which the lateral background concentration in condition (5) was taken from CAMS, uniformly distributed up to 1500 m above ground.

The purpose of this comparison should be stated explicitly. The paper introduces a formulation, not an operational forecasting system: the emission coefficient has not been calibrated, and the local configuration deliberately excludes dust transported into the domain. The comparison therefore tests the aspects that follow from the formulation—the timing of the event and, above all, the size composition—rather than the absolute concentration level.

At Bukhara the model reproduces the onset of the storm on the evening of 28 April within about an hour and the temporal evolution with a correlation of 0.50 (0.56 with nesting), comparable to that of CAMS (0.64). All configurations overestimate the storm-day mean excess at Bukhara, by factors of 1.4 (local), 2.2 (nested) and 1.7 (CAMS). The most distinctive result concerns the size composition. During the storm the observed PM~2.5~/PM~10~ ratio varied between 0.13 and 0.21 (median 0.16). The fraction-resolved model with local emission gives 0.13, within the observed range, whereas CAMS gives 0.48, more than twice the largest observed value. The observations show that the storm dust, and even the urban background, consists mainly of coarse mineral particles, while CAMS overestimates the fine fraction and underestimates the background by a factor of two.

With nesting, the statistics over the three stations become comparable to or slightly better than those of CAMS, but the fine-mode bias of CAMS is imported: the PM~2.5~/PM~10~ ratio at Bukhara rises to 0.23. The size composition thus provides the clearest observational support for the size resolution that is the subject of this paper; a detailed regional evaluation, including emission calibration, is in preparation.

# Discussion and Limitations

The experiment confirms the internal consistency of the model and its physically meaningful response to the controlling parameters, and it shows a clear benefit of size resolution. The following limitations should be kept in mind.

First, the prefactor of the sandblasting efficiency (13) acts as an effective emission coefficient, and the clay fraction to which the emission is most sensitive after wind speed had to be taken from the literature for the desert. The overestimation of the storm-day mean at Bukhara by a factor of 1.4 is consistent with an emission bias of the sources located directly upwind of the station, on the northern margin of the oasis (Fig. 6A). Systematic calibration of the emission coefficient on a set of events and independent verification on another set is planned.

Second, the stability parameters in Eq. (17) are fixed at typical daytime values, so that the diurnal cycle of turbulent mixing is not represented; this is a likely reason why the model disperses the dust more slowly than observed after the storm (Fig. 10A).

Third, Stokes' law overestimates the settling velocity of the coarsest fraction by about 20%, which affects the near-source deposition only.

Fourth, the ERA5-Land soil moisture refers to the 0–7 cm layer, whereas erosion is controlled by the uppermost millimetres. In a winter event (February 2026) the model produced no emission because the reanalysis moisture exceeded the threshold, although dust was observed at Bukhara. A correction for the moisture of the surface layer is therefore needed for cold-season applications.

Fifth, the model represents the 6-km cell average, whereas urban stations are also influenced by sub-grid local sources, which limits the point-wise comparison.

# Conclusions

A three-dimensional non-stationary model of wind erosion and atmospheric dust loading has been developed in which emission and transport form a single boundary-value problem. The vertical detachment flux enters the formulation as a boundary condition of the third kind at the underlying surface, where it is balanced by dry deposition. Emission and deposition are therefore described by one boundary relation, the direction of the surface exchange follows from the sign of their difference, and the regime transition is continuous. The dust is resolved into transport fractions whose settling velocities differ by more than four orders of magnitude.

The transport equation is written in a generalized form that admits the actual particle velocity obtained from the relaxation equations; the classical form is its limiting case. For the PM~2.5~ and PM~10~ fractions the particle lag behind the flow is negligible, and it becomes noticeable only for particles of several tens of micrometres near the source. This methodological result delimits the range in which the classical form is sufficient.

All surface-state factors are combined into a single effective threshold friction velocity. The properties of the model—the integral mass budget, non-negativity, continuity of the surface flux with respect to the friction velocity, limiting cases and the governing dimensionless numbers—have been established analytically.

A 48-h simulation of the dust storm of 28–29 April 2025 over the Bukhara region with reanalysis meteorology and global soil and land-cover data satisfied the mass budget to within 0.003%. A single effective mode with a priori diameters underestimated dry deposition by a factor of 3.6–4.9 and overestimated the near-surface concentration by about 20%. A diameter tuned to the deposition (29.9 µm) reproduced the bulk budget to within 10%, but it is not known a priori and provides no PM~10~ and PM~2.5~ concentrations, which constitute less than half of the near-surface dust mass. Wind speed and soil clay content were found to be the dominant parameters. Compared with station observations, the model reproduced the onset and evolution of the storm at Bukhara with a skill comparable to the operational CAMS reanalysis. It also reproduced the observed PM~2.5~/PM~10~ ratio of storm dust (0.13 against 0.13–0.21 observed), where CAMS gave 0.48. Further work will address the calibration of the emission coefficient, a time-dependent boundary-layer stability and a surface-moisture correction.

# Appendix A: Sensitivity to the Grid Spacing and Time Step {-}

To assess the numerical robustness of the results of Section 8, the 48-h simulation was repeated with the time step halved (Δ*t* = 60 s) and with the horizontal grid spacing halved (120 × 120 nodes, Δ*x* = 3.2 km, Δ*y* = 3.3 km); in the latter case the input fields were interpolated from the same source data onto the finer grid. The results are compared in Table A1.

::: tablecaption
Table A1: Sensitivity of the 48-h results to the time step and horizontal grid spacing
:::

| Quantity | Base (60 × 60, Δ*t* = 120 s) | Δ*t* = 60 s | 120 × 120, Δ*t* = 120 s |
|---|---|---|---|
| Emitted (kt) | 1000.8 | 1000.8 (0.0%) | 921.4 (−7.9%) |
| Dry-deposited, share of emission (%) | 37.2 | 37.3 | 36.8 |
| Exported, share of emission (%) | 62.1 | 62.1 | 62.6 |
| 48-h mean near-surface concentration (µg/m^3^) | 205 | 204 (−0.2%) | 189 (−7.5%) |
| Budget residual (%) | 0.0029 | 0.0014 | 0.0029 |

Note: Values in parentheses are differences relative to the base configuration.

Halving the time step changes all quantities by less than 1%. Halving the grid spacing reduces the emitted mass by 7.9%, which results from the finer representation of the input fields: because of the threshold character of the emission (12), averaging the wind and surface parameters over coarser cells does not preserve the emission. The partition of the emitted mass between deposition and export, which is determined by the transport part of the model, changes by less than 0.5 percentage points, and the change of the mean concentration (−7.5%) follows the change of the emission. The transport solution is thus robust with respect to both discretization parameters, whereas the grid dependence of the emission is a property of the input resolution that should be taken into account when the emission coefficient is calibrated.
