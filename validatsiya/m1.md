::: abstract
**ABSTRACT:** Wind erosion and the resulting atmospheric dust loading are major environmental problems of the arid territories of Central Asia. Most existing models treat the detachment of particles from the soil surface and their transport by the air flow separately: the emission flux is computed by a stand-alone scheme and inserted into the transport equation as a volume source. This paper presents a three-dimensional non-stationary model in which both stages form a single boundary-value problem. The vertical dust flux, computed by a two-step saltation–sandblasting scheme, enters the formulation as a boundary condition of the third kind at the underlying surface, where it is balanced by dry deposition; the direction of the net surface exchange then follows from the sign of the difference between emission and deposition, and the transition between the two regimes is continuous. The dust is resolved into transport fractions whose gravitational settling velocities differ by more than four orders of magnitude. All surface-state factors are combined into a single effective threshold friction velocity. The transport equation is written in a generalized form in which advection is performed with the actual particle velocity obtained from the particle relaxation equations; the classical form with the air velocity and a separate settling term is recovered as a limiting case, and quantitative bounds of its applicability are given. The mass budget, non-negativity, continuity of the regime transition and the governing dimensionless numbers are established analytically. A 48-hour simulation of the dust storm of 28–29 April 2025 over the Bukhara region (Uzbekistan), driven by reanalysis meteorology and global soil and land-cover data, shows that a single effective mode with an a priori diameter (4.5–10 µm) underestimates dry deposition by a factor of 3.6–4.9 and overestimates the near-surface concentration by about 20%. A diameter tuned to reproduce the deposition (29.9 µm) recovers the bulk budget and the total concentration to within 10%, but it can only be determined from a size-resolved solution and provides no PM~10~ or PM~2.5~ concentrations, which make up less than half of the near-surface dust mass. Compared with ground-based observations, the fraction-resolved model reproduces the PM~2.5~/PM~10~ ratio of storm dust (0.13 vs. an observed 0.13–0.21), whereas the operational Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) reanalysis gives 0.48.

# Introduction

Arid and semi-arid lands cover more than one third of the continental surface and are the main source of mineral aerosol in the atmosphere [@shao2011cycle;@prospero2002;@ginoux2012]. Wind erosion removes the finest and most fertile soil fraction, and the entrained dust is transported over hundreds to thousands of kilometres, altering the radiation balance, degrading air quality and depositing far from the source. In Central Asia the problem has been aggravated by the desiccation of the Aral Sea: the exposed seabed, the Aralkum, has become a significant anthropogenic dust source that adds about 7% per year to the total dust amount over the region [@banks2022]; ground-based monitoring in the Turan lowland has nevertheless shown the Kyzylkum desert to be a far more active dust source than the Aralkum [@opp2019], and dust storms are recorded at meteorological stations across Uzbekistan on up to several tens of days per year, with more than 30 days per year in the Bukhara region [@indoitu2012;@rakhmatova2025;@nishonov2023].

Several practical tasks require quantitative forecasts of dust concentration fields. Public health services need forecasts of exceedances of air-quality limits for the PM~10~ and PM~2.5~ fractions at least one day ahead. Agriculture needs estimates of topsoil loss from specific fields in order to plan shelterbelts and tillage practices, which cannot be measured over entire territories. Planning of sand fixation and phytomelioration projects requires scenario calculations of how emission changes when particular areas are stabilized. Infrastructure operators need estimates of visibility and dust loading for roads, airports and solar power plants. In all these cases in situ measurements provide values at individual points and times, whereas decisions require the concentration field over the whole territory and its evolution; the only tool that provides such a picture is mathematical modelling.

Physically the process consists of two stages. First, particles are detached from the soil surface and lifted into the surface layer; the controlling factors are the threshold friction velocity, soil moisture, particle size distribution, roughness elements and aerodynamic roughness. Second, the lifted particles are carried by the air flow and dispersed by turbulent diffusion, and the result is controlled by the turbulence regime of the boundary layer, gravitational settling and orography.

Existing models describe both stages but, as shown in Section 2, usually separately: the emission flux is computed by a dedicated scheme and passed to the transport model as a prescribed volume source. In addition, the particle size distribution is either reduced to a single effective mode or represented by fractions with fixed settling velocities, although both the settling velocity and the horizontal particle velocity components are formed by the wind field and vary with it.

This work addresses these points. Its contributions are as follows:

1. Emission and transport are combined into one boundary-value problem: the detachment flux enters as a boundary condition of the third kind at the underlying surface, where it is balanced by dry deposition. The same boundary relation describes emission and deposition, so that the transition between the two regimes is continuous and requires no switching between models, and no injection layer has to be chosen for the emission flux.
2. The emission is resolved into transport fractions, each with its own settling velocity and dry-deposition velocity. All surface-state factors are combined into a single effective threshold velocity, which makes the model configurable from available soil maps and remote sensing data and simplifies sensitivity analysis. It is shown that a single effective mode can reproduce the bulk mass budget only when its diameter is tuned against a size-resolved solution, and that it cannot provide the regulated PM~10~ and PM~2.5~ fractions.
3. The transport equation is written in a generalized form in which advection is performed with the actual particle velocity obtained from the particle relaxation equations. The classical form is a limiting case, and the particle sizes and distances for which it is sufficient are established quantitatively. This result is methodological: it delimits the range of applicability of the classical form rather than replacing it.
4. The properties of the model are established analytically: the integral mass budget, the non-negativity of the solution, the continuity of the regime transition, limiting cases and dimensionless numbers that determine the transport regime.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the state of research. Section 3 formulates the problem and the assumptions. Section 4 derives the model, Section 5 presents the parameterizations, and Section 6 establishes the properties of the model. Section 7 outlines the numerical implementation, Section 8 presents a computational experiment for a real dust storm, Section 9 discusses the limitations, and Section 10 concludes; Appendix A examines the sensitivity to the grid spacing and time step.

# State of Research

**Physics of particle entrainment.** The quantitative description of wind erosion goes back to the experiments of Bagnold [@bagnold1941], who identified saltation as the main transport mechanism of sand and related the horizontal saltation flux to the cube of the friction velocity. Owen [@owen1964] explained the self-regulation of the saltation layer through the change of effective roughness; similar expressions for the horizontal flux were obtained by Kawamura [@kawamura1951] and White [@white1979], and early systematic experiments were performed by Chepil [@chepil1945]. Later work moved from the horizontal saltation flux to the vertical flux of fine dust, which determines atmospheric dust loading. Two lines of research emerged. The first relates the vertical flux to the horizontal flux through an empirical efficiency coefficient that depends on the clay content [@mb1995;@gillette1988]. The second is based on the bombardment of the surface by saltating grains, with explicit description of the ejection and disaggregation of soil aggregates [@shao1993;@alfaro1997;@alfaro2001;@shao2008]. The second line is physically more consistent, as confirmed later [@kok2012;@kok2014;@shao2011size], but requires data on aggregate binding energies that are rarely available for a specific territory. The present work adopts the first approach, complemented by size resolution of the emitted dust.

**Threshold and surface state.** The dependence of the threshold friction velocity on particle diameter, with a minimum in the range of about 75–100 µm, was established experimentally [@iversen1982] and received a compact analytical expression [@shaolu2000]: for smaller particles the threshold increases because of interparticle cohesion, for larger ones because of weight. The effect of soil moisture is described by the correction of Fécan et al. [@fecan1999], which accounts for capillary bridges between grains and acts only above a residual moisture that itself depends on the clay content. The effect of vegetation and non-erodible roughness elements is introduced through the drag-partition scheme of Raupach et al. [@raupach1993], and field estimates of threshold friction velocity for arid lands are systematized in [@mb1997]. The size distribution of erodible soils is represented by a sum of lognormal modes [@chatenet1996]. These three corrections were developed independently, and the legitimacy of their multiplicative combination remains a matter of discussion; the combination is adopted here and its consequences are examined in Sections 6 and 8.

**Classes of models.** Empirical models rely on regression relations obtained in field and wind-tunnel experiments, the classical example being the wind erosion equation [@woodruff1965] and its revised versions [@fryrear2000;@fryrear1995]. They are simple and fast, but their coefficients are valid only for the conditions of the underlying experiments, and they do not provide the space–time distribution of concentration at all. Semi-empirical schemes describe the physical mechanisms theoretically and parameterize individual coefficients from observations [@mb1995;@shao1993;@alfaro1997]; they are more universal but address emission rather than transport. Deterministic models describe transport by the advection–diffusion equation that follows from mass conservation [@marchuk1982] and remain the only class that provides the concentration field in the whole domain. The Eulerian approach yields this field directly on a grid [@ginoux2001], whereas the Lagrangian approach follows trajectories and is convenient for point sources and complex orography but requires many trajectories for stable statistics [@stein2015]. For an area source such as an eroding surface the Eulerian approach is preferable, and it is adopted here.

**Modelling systems.** The best known systems are HYSPLIT [@stein2015], CHIMERE [@menut2013] and WRF-Chem [@grell2005]. They couple emission and transport within mature platforms, have been extensively evaluated and perform well at synoptic scales. Their limitations for the present purposes are methodological rather than computational: their emission parameterizations are fixed and tuned mainly to the Sahara or the deserts of East Asia, their internal schemes are difficult to modify, and the size distribution is prescribed by a predefined set of modes. Regional studies note that such settings are not directly transferable to the specific dust and salt-laden material of the Aral Sea region [@indoitu2012;@opp2017]; a recent inventory also shows the large number and diversity of dust sources in Central Asia [@nobakht2021]. The closure relations used here—dry deposition by the resistance method [@wesely1989;@slinn1982;@seinfeld2016], stability functions of the surface layer [@businger1971;@stull1988], and the terrain-following coordinate [@phillips1957] with its known accuracy limitations [@mesinger1982]—are taken from established practice; well-posedness of problems of this class relies on the theory of parabolic equations [@ladyzhenskaya1968], and the input fields are available from modern reanalyses [@munoz2021;@hersbach2020].

**Previous work of the authors.** The present work continues earlier studies of the authors. In [@ravshanov2019] a model of aerosol particle dispersion was constructed in which particle velocities depend on the particle mass, diameter and density; a nonlinear model for monitoring and forecasting harmful substances in the atmosphere and the influence of vegetation cover on transport were studied in [@shafiev2021;@shafiyev2023], and efficient splitting-based algorithms for regional pollution problems were developed in [@shafiev2024;@ravshanov2020]. These works considered industrial and generic aerosol sources; the coupling with a physically based wind-erosion emission scheme and the size-resolved treatment of mineral dust are new here.

**Open problem.** The comparison of the above directions shows that emission and transport are almost always separated: the emission flux is computed by a stand-alone block following [@mb1995;@gillette1988;@shao1993;@alfaro1997;@alfaro2001] and passed to the transport model as a volume source in the surface layer, which requires the choice of an injection layer and treats emission and deposition by different model components. The size distribution, if resolved, is described by fractions with fixed settling velocities, whereas the variability of these velocities follows from the physics of the process [@ravshanov2019;@shafiev2021]. Removing these two gaps is the subject of this paper.

# Problem Statement

The atmospheric domain over an arid territory is a rectangular parallelepiped whose lower face coincides with the underlying surface and whose upper face lies within the boundary layer, above the level at which the contribution of surface emission is practically exhausted. The problem is to find the space–time distribution of the mass concentration of dust particles of each fraction for a given wind field, surface state and soil size distribution.

The particles are divided into *N*~f~ fractions by equivalent diameter. The division reflects the fact that the regime of particle motion is determined by its size: coarse particles (larger than about 500 µm) move by creep and practically do not enter atmospheric transport, intermediate particles move by saltation, and fine particles (smaller than about 70 µm) become suspended and are transported over large distances [@bagnold1941;@shao2008;@kok2012]. The subject of modelling are the suspendable fractions; saltation is treated as the mechanism that supplies them.

The following assumptions are adopted.

1. Air is treated as an incompressible medium; the wind field is prescribed and satisfies the continuity equation. The feedback of dust on the flow is neglected, which is justified at the volume concentrations typical of the conditions considered.
2. Particles are spherical.
3. Fractions interact with the flow independently: coagulation and fragmentation are not considered, which is admissible at moderate concentrations and relatively short residence times.
4. Turbulent transport is described by gradient (K-theory) closure with a diagonal diffusion tensor aligned with the coordinate axes [@marchuk1982;@stull1988].
5. Wet deposition and chemical transformations are neglected, which is acceptable for arid territories with few precipitation days; dry deposition is prescribed by the surface boundary condition.

These assumptions restrict the model to regional and local problems in the absence of intense precipitation but retain a physically meaningful description of the size distribution and of variable particle velocities, which is the purpose of the model.
