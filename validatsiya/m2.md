# Mathematical Model

The model is based on the conservation of mass of the admixture [@marchuk1982]. The main difference from the conventional formulation is the way emission is coupled with transport: the detachment flux enters not as a volume source but as a boundary condition at the underlying surface. In addition, the transport equation is written in a generalized form that admits the actual particle velocity; the classical form remains a limiting case.

## Transport Equation

For the concentration of the *i*-th fraction in Ω × (0, *T*],

$$\frac{\partial C_i}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial (v_{x,i}C_i)}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial (v_{y,i}C_i)}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial (v_{z,i}C_i)}{\partial z}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(K_x\frac{\partial C_i}{\partial x}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(K_y\frac{\partial C_i}{\partial y}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(K_z\frac{\partial C_i}{\partial z}\right)-\sigma_i C_i+f_i, \qquad (1)$$

where *C*~*i*~ is the mass concentration of the *i*-th fraction (kg/m^3^), *i* = 1, …, *N*~f~; *v*~*x,i*~, *v*~*y,i*~, *v*~*z,i*~ are the components of the particle transport velocity (m/s); *K*~*x*~, *K*~*y*~, *K*~*z*~ are the turbulent diffusion coefficients (m^2^/s); σ~*i*~ is the removal coefficient accounting for scavenging (s^−1^); and *f*~*i*~ represents volume sources of non-erosive origin (kg m^−3^ s^−1^).

## Particle Velocities

In most models the particle is assumed to move with the air velocity, and settling is introduced as a separate term. This neglects inertia: a particle leaving the surface does not acquire the flow velocity instantaneously. To delimit the range in which this simplification is valid, the particle velocity is determined explicitly [@ravshanov2019]. The motion of a spherical particle of diameter *d*~*i*~ and density ρ~*p*~ is described by Newton's second law with Stokes drag,

$$\frac{d\mathbf{v}_i}{dt}=\frac{\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{v}_i}{\tau_{p,i}}+\mathbf{g}, \qquad \tau_{p,i}=\frac{\rho_p d_i^{2}}{18\mu}, \qquad (2)$$

where **U** is the wind velocity, **v**~*i*~ the particle velocity, μ the dynamic viscosity of air and τ~*p,i*~ the relaxation time. The initial condition reflects the discrete nature of detachment: the particle rests on the surface until the moment of entrainment and then relaxes towards the local flow velocity. In the implementation, system (2) is integrated at every transport time step by the three-stage scheme of Crank–Nicolson and BDF2 type [@cranknicolson1947] implemented in the DustVel program [@dustvel]; the scheme remains stable for the stiff system formed by fractions whose relaxation times differ by four orders of magnitude.

**Classical formulation as a limiting case.** For *v*~*x,i*~ = *u*, *v*~*y,i*~ = *v* and *v*~*z,i*~ = *w* − *w*~*g,i*~, Eq. (1) reduces to the conventional form, in which advection is performed with the air velocity and settling with velocity *w*~*g,i*~ is a separate term. The proposed model therefore contains the classical one [@ginoux2001;@marchuk1982] as a special case.

**Limits of applicability of the simplification.** Since the relaxation time is proportional to the square of the diameter, it differs by orders of magnitude between fractions. For particles of 2.5 µm (ρ~*p*~ = 1500 kg/m^3^), 10 µm (2000 kg/m^3^) and 50 µm (2500 kg/m^3^) it equals 0.029 ms, 0.61 ms and 19.2 ms, respectively [@dustvel]. At a wind speed of 10 m/s the corresponding horizontal lag *U*τ~*p*~ is 0.3 mm, 6.1 mm and 19 cm, i.e. negligible compared with any practical grid spacing. The inertial correction therefore matters only within the first metres after lift-off and for particles of several tens of micrometres and larger; for regional transport of the fine fractions the classical form is sufficient. This conclusion is quantified by the Stokes number in Section 6.

## Surface Boundary Condition

The key element of the formulation is the way emission is introduced. Instead of a volume source in the lowest grid layer, a boundary condition of the third kind is imposed at the underlying surface, relating the diffusive flux to the emission and dry-deposition fluxes:

$$K_z\left.\frac{\partial C_i}{\partial z}\right|_{z=h(x,y)}=-F_i+v_{d,i}\left.C_i\right|_{z=h(x,y)}, \qquad (3)$$

where *F*~*i*~ (kg m^−2^ s^−1^) is the emission flux of the *i*-th fraction, *v*~*d,i*~ the dry-deposition velocity and *h*(*x*, *y*) the terrain height. The first term on the right describes dust lifted by wind erosion, the second the return of particles to the surface. It is through this condition that the two stages of the process are combined into a single mathematical problem.

This formulation has two advantages over a volume source. First, the surface exchange is prescribed as a flux, so that no injection layer has to be chosen, and emission and deposition are treated by the same boundary relation: the sign of *F*~*i*~ − *v*~*d,i*~*C*~*i*~ determines whether the surface acts as a source or a sink at a given moment. Second, when the friction velocity falls below the threshold, *F*~*i*~ vanishes and condition (3) becomes a pure deposition condition; the regime change occurs continuously, without switching between models.

## Initial, Lateral and Top Conditions

At the initial moment a background distribution is prescribed:

$$C_i(x,y,z,0)=C_i^{0}(x,y,z), \qquad (x,y,z)\in\Omega. \qquad (4)$$

At the lateral and top boundaries, conditions of the third kind describe the exchange with the surrounding atmosphere:

$$K_n\left.\frac{\partial C_i}{\partial n}\right|_{\Gamma}=\gamma_i\left(C_i-C_i^{\mathrm{bg}}\right)_{\Gamma}, \qquad (5)$$

where Γ is the corresponding part of the boundary, *n* its outward normal, γ~*i*~ the exchange coefficient and $C_i^{\mathrm{bg}}$ the background concentration of the inflowing air. Condition (5) covers both limiting cases: γ~*i*~ = 0 gives a zero-flux condition, and γ~*i*~ → ∞ a prescribed concentration. This makes it possible to adapt the model to different physical situations without changing its structure [@shafiev2021], including nesting into a larger-scale model through $C_i^{\mathrm{bg}}$.

## Terrain-Following Coordinate

Terrain makes the lower boundary curvilinear, which hinders the direct use of finite-difference methods. The transformation [@phillips1957]

$$\zeta=H\,\frac{z-h(x,y)}{H-h(x,y)} \qquad (6)$$

maps the domain onto a rectangular parallelepiped: the ground becomes the plane ζ = 0 and the top the plane ζ = *H*. The structure of Eq. (1) is preserved, but metric terms depending on the terrain gradients appear. The transformation has a known drawback: in regions of steep terrain the metric coefficients grow and the accuracy of horizontal gradients decreases [@mesinger1982]. The top *H* is kept constant in time: if it were identified with the diurnally varying boundary-layer depth, the coordinate would become time-dependent and an additional advective term would appear. Instead, *H* is chosen well above the expected maximum boundary-layer depth.

## Linear and Nonlinear Variants

In the linear variant the coefficients of Eq. (1) do not depend on concentration, the emission flux is a prescribed function of time and the fractions are independent, so that the system splits into *N*~f~ separate problems. In the nonlinear variant the particle velocities are obtained from system (2) and depend on the wind field, and the emission flux is a strongly nonlinear function of the friction velocity that changes with soil moisture; the nonlinearity of the problem is therefore concentrated at the boundary. Together with the parameterizations of Section 5, Eqs. (1)–(6) form a closed formulation (Fig. 1).

# Parameterization of the Controlling Factors

Closure of the model requires the emission flux entering condition (3), the turbulent diffusion coefficients and the settling and dry-deposition velocities of each fraction. The emission scheme is the key element and is constructed in two steps: first the saltation flux is computed, then it is converted into the vertical flux of fine dust.

## Effective Threshold Friction Velocity

Fine particles (*d* < 20 µm) are not detached from the surface directly because of cohesion [@shao1993;@kok2012]. Their main source is the impact of saltating sand grains, which break soil aggregates and eject fine material. The calculation therefore starts from the threshold of saltating grains. All surface-state factors are combined into one effective value,

$$u_{*t}^{\mathrm{eff}}=u_{*t}^{0}(d_s)\,f_w(w)\,R_\lambda(\lambda), \qquad (7)$$

where $u_{*t}^{0}$ is the threshold for a dry bare surface given by the expression of Shao and Lu [@shaolu2000],

$$u_{*t}^{0}(d)=\sqrt{A_N\left(\frac{\rho_p g d}{\rho_a}+\frac{\gamma}{\rho_a d}\right)}, \qquad (8)$$

with *A*~*N*~ = 0.0123, γ = 3 × 10^−4^ kg/s^2^, ρ~*p*~ = 2650 kg/m^3^ and air density ρ~*a*~ = 1.225 kg/m^3^. Expression (8) has its minimum of 0.237 m/s at *d* ≈ 107 µm (Fig. 2A). The threshold is evaluated at the reference saltation diameter *d*~*s*~ = 75 µm, close to the experimentally observed minimum [@iversen1982], where it equals 0.244 m/s, i.e. 3% above the minimum of (8).

The factor *f*~*w*~ accounts for soil moisture [@fecan1999]:

$$f_w=\begin{cases}1, & w\le w',\\ \sqrt{1+1.21\,(w-w')^{0.68}}, & w>w',\end{cases} \qquad w'=0.0014\,c^{2}+0.17\,c, \qquad (9)$$

where *w* is the gravimetric soil moisture (%) and *c* the clay content (%). The factor *R*~λ~ describes the partition of shear stress between the erodible surface and roughness elements (vegetation and non-erodible elements) [@raupach1993]:

$$R_\lambda=\sqrt{(1-m\sigma_r\lambda)(1+m\beta_r\lambda)}, \qquad (10)$$

with β~*r*~ = 90, σ~*r*~ = 1 and *m* = 0.5, where λ is the roughness density (frontal area index). Both factors are not smaller than unity, i.e. they only raise the threshold (Fig. 2B,C). The methodological advantage of form (7) is that all surface-state parameters are collected in a single effective value and can be varied uniformly.

## From Saltation to Vertical Dust Flux

The friction velocity is obtained from the logarithmic profile,

$$u_*=\frac{\kappa U_{10}}{\ln(z_{\mathrm{ref}}/z_0)}, \qquad (11)$$

with κ = 0.4, *z*~ref~ = 10 m and aerodynamic roughness length *z*~0~. The horizontal saltation flux is given by the expression of White [@white1979]:

$$Q=C_W\frac{\rho_a}{g}\,u_*^{3}\left(1-\frac{u_{*t}^{\mathrm{eff}}}{u_*}\right)\left(1+\frac{u_{*t}^{\mathrm{eff}}}{u_*}\right)^{2}, \qquad u_*>u_{*t}^{\mathrm{eff}}, \qquad (12)$$

with *C*~*W*~ = 2.61 and *Q* = 0 otherwise. The vertical dust flux is obtained through the sandblasting efficiency coefficient α, which grows exponentially with clay content [@mb1995], and the erodible fraction *A*~ef~ of the grid cell:

$$F=\alpha\,Q\,A_{\mathrm{ef}}, \qquad \alpha=10^{\,13.4\,m_{cl}-6}\ \mathrm{m}^{-1}, \qquad m_{cl}\le 0.2, \qquad (13)$$

where *m*~*cl*~ is the clay mass fraction; the cap reflects the saturation of the efficiency for clay-rich soils, whose aggregates are stronger. The exponential clay dependence in Eq. (13) follows Marticorena and Bergametti [@mb1995]; its dimensional prefactor is two orders of magnitude smaller than in the original relation, where α is expressed in cm^−1^. The prefactor therefore acts as an effective emission-efficiency coefficient for the surfaces considered; it was fixed before the observations of Section 8.7 were analysed and was not tuned to them. With the original prefactor the emission, and hence the simulated dust concentration, would be about one hundred times larger. For the storm of Section 8 this would exceed the observed storm-day excess at Bukhara by about two orders of magnitude, whereas the adopted value reproduces it to within a factor of 1.4 (Section 8.7). The resulting dependence of *F* on *u*~*~ is shown in Fig. 3.

## Size-Resolved Emission

The total flux is insufficient, because particles of different size differ in settling velocity by orders of magnitude and follow completely different paths. The mass size distribution of the emitted dust is described by a sum of three lognormal modes,

$$\frac{dM}{d\ln d}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{\omega_j}{\sqrt{2\pi}\ln\sigma_j}\exp\left[-\frac{\ln^{2}(d/D_j)}{2\ln^{2}\sigma_j}\right], \qquad (14)$$

with median diameters *D*~*j*~ = 1.5, 6.7 and 45 µm, geometric standard deviations σ~*j*~ = 1.7, 1.6 and 1.5 and weights ω~*j*~ = 0.05, 0.40 and 0.55. The first two modes follow the emitted-aerosol modes of Alfaro et al. [@alfaro1997;@alfaro2001]; the third, coarse mode represents the short-range suspended fraction produced by sandblasting of sandy desert soils. Integration of (14) over the transport fractions gives the share *p*~*i*~ of each fraction and the fractional flux *F*~*i*~ = *p*~*i*~*F* entering condition (3). Five transport fractions are used (Table 1, Fig. 4A).

## Settling and Dry Deposition

The gravitational settling velocity follows from the balance of gravity and Stokes drag,

$$w_{g,i}=\frac{\rho_{p,i}\,g\,d_i^{2}}{18\mu}, \qquad (15)$$

evaluated at the representative diameter of each fraction. The values span more than four orders of magnitude, from 0.0023 cm/s to 39 cm/s (Table 1, Fig. 4B), which explains quantitatively why fine fractions travel far while coarse ones settle near the source. Stokes' law is valid for particle Reynolds numbers below about unity; for the coarsest fraction Re~*p*~ ≈ 1.8, and the standard drag correction would reduce its settling velocity by about 20%. This affects only the fraction that is deposited within a few kilometres of the source (Section 9).

![Figure 4: (A) Mass size distribution of emitted dust, Eq. (14), with fraction bounds (dashed) and mass shares; (B) settling velocity of the transport fractions, Eq. (15)](fig/Fig4.png)

The dry-deposition velocity is computed by the resistance method [@wesely1989;@slinn1982;@seinfeld2016]:

$$v_{d,i}=w_{g,i}+\frac{1}{r_a+r_b+r_a r_b w_{g,i}}, \qquad r_a=\frac{\ln(z_{\mathrm{ref}}/z_0)}{\kappa u_*}, \qquad (16)$$

where *r*~*a*~ is the aerodynamic resistance (averaged over the domain) and *r*~*b*~ = 50 s/m the quasi-laminar sublayer resistance.

## Turbulent Diffusion and Wind Field

The vertical diffusion coefficient follows Monin–Obukhov similarity with a boundary-layer profile [@businger1971;@stull1988]:

$$K_z=\frac{\kappa u_* z}{\varphi_h(z/L)}\left(1-\frac{z}{h_{\mathrm{bl}}}\right)^{2}, \qquad \varphi_h=\begin{cases}1+5\,z/L, & z/L\ge 0,\\ (1-16\,z/L)^{-1/2}, & z/L<0,\end{cases} \qquad (17)$$

limited to 0.1 m^2^/s ≤ *K*~*z*~ ≤ 200 m^2^/s. In the present computations the Obukhov length *L* = −50 m and the boundary-layer depth *h*~bl~ = 1500 m are fixed, representing the unstable daytime conditions typical of dust events; the horizontal coefficients are *K*~*x*~ = *K*~*y*~ = 10^3^ m^2^/s. The horizontal wind follows a power-law profile, **U**~h~(*z*) = **U**~10~(*z*/*z*~ref~)^0.16^, with the 10-m wind taken from reanalysis, and the vertical component is recovered from the continuity equation,

$$w(z)=-\int_{h}^{z}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}\right)dz', \qquad (18)$$

which ensures conservation of air mass in the flow field.

# Properties of the Model

The formulation has several properties that determine its range of applicability and serve as correctness criteria for computations. They are derived here analytically.

**Integral mass budget.** Integrating Eq. (1) over Ω and applying the divergence theorem with the incompressibility of air and conditions (3) and (5) gives, for each fraction,

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}C_i\,d\Omega=\int_{S}F_i\,dS-\int_{S}v_{d,i}C_i\,dS-\oint_{\Gamma}\gamma_i\left(C_i-C_i^{\mathrm{bg}}\right)d\Gamma-\sigma_i\int_{\Omega}C_i\,d\Omega+\int_{\Omega}f_i\,d\Omega, \qquad (19)$$

where *S* is the underlying surface and Γ the lateral and top boundaries. The change of mass in the domain equals the input by emission minus dry deposition, exchange through the outer boundaries and volume removal. Emission enters as a surface integral determined by the surface state only; relation (19) also serves as a test of the numerical algorithm, whose budget residual must not exceed the scheme error.

**Non-negativity.** If the initial distribution, the background concentration of the inflowing air and the sources are non-negative, the solution remains non-negative for all *t* > 0. This follows from the maximum principle for parabolic equations [@ladyzhenskaya1968]: at a point of a negative interior minimum the time derivative and the diffusion term have signs incompatible with the equation. A negative minimum is also impossible at the surface: for *C*~*i*~ = 0 condition (3) gives a non-negative diffusive flux directed into the domain, because *F*~*i*~ ≥ 0. A numerical scheme should inherit this property; the discrete behaviour is discussed in Section 7.

**Continuity of the regime transition.** The emission flux is defined piecewise and vanishes for *u*~*~ ≤ $u_{*t}^{\mathrm{eff}}$. By Eq. (12), *Q* = *C*~*W*~ρ~*a*~*g*^−1^(*u*~*~ − *u*~*t*~)(*u*~*~ + *u*~*t*~)^2^ above the threshold; it tends to zero at the threshold, so that *F*~*i*~ is a continuous, piecewise-smooth and locally Lipschitz function of the friction velocity (its derivative jumps from 0 to 4*C*~*W*~ρ~*a*~*u*~*t*~^2^/*g* at the threshold). The right-hand side of condition (3) is therefore continuous, and when the wind fluctuates near the threshold—a typical situation at the beginning and end of a dust storm—the surface flux does not jump. This is what allows emission and deposition to be described by one condition without switching between models.

**Dimensionless numbers.** Scaling Eq. (1) with a horizontal scale *L*~*x*~, a velocity scale *U* and the boundary-layer depth *H*~bl~, the transport regime is determined by three numbers:

$$\mathrm{Pe}=\frac{UL_x}{K_x}, \qquad \mathrm{Se}_i=\frac{w_{g,i}L_x}{UH_{\mathrm{bl}}}, \qquad \mathrm{St}_i=\frac{\tau_{p,i}U}{L_x}. \qquad (20)$$

For the regional problem of Section 8 (*U* = 10 m/s, *L*~*x*~ = 380 km, *K*~*x*~ = 10^3^ m^2^/s, *H*~bl~ = 1.5 km) the Péclet number is about 4 × 10^3^, and the grid Péclet number *U*Δ*x*/*K*~*x*~ is about 60, i.e. transport is strongly advection-dominated. This has a direct consequence for the algorithm: schemes with central differences for the convective terms lose monotonicity at such Péclet numbers [@samarskii2001]. The settling parameter Se~*i*~ shows whether a particle settles within the domain length: it ranges from 6 × 10^−4^ for the finest to about 10 for the coarsest fraction, which quantitatively justifies abandoning a single-mode description. The Stokes number St~*i*~ does not exceed 10^−4^ at the grid scale for any fraction, so that the simplification **v**~*i*~ = **U** is justified for regional transport, in agreement with the estimates of Section 4.2.

**Limiting cases.** The model contains known formulations as special cases, which serves as a consistency check. For *v*~*x,i*~ = *u*, *v*~*y,i*~ = *v*, *v*~*z,i*~ = *w* − *w*~*g,i*~ Eq. (1) reduces to the classical form with a separate settling term. For γ~*i*~ = 0 condition (5) gives zero flux through the boundary, and for γ~*i*~ → ∞ a prescribed concentration. For *N*~f~ = 1, constant coefficients, flat terrain, a steady uniform flow and a point source instead of areal emission the solution reduces to the classical Gaussian plume. For *u*~*~ below the threshold, condition (3) becomes a pure deposition condition. These transitions allow the numerical algorithm to be verified step by step, starting from problems with known solutions.

# Numerical Implementation

The model is implemented in the DustFlow program complex. A detailed analysis of the numerical algorithm is in preparation; its main features are summarized here, and the sensitivity of the results to the grid spacing and time step is shown in Appendix A. The transport equation is written in conservative form in the terrain-following coordinate (6), with the metric factor *H* − *h* included in the fluxes. Directional splitting reduces each time step to a sequence of one-dimensional problems in *x*, *y* and ζ [@marchuk1988;@yanenko1971]. In each one-dimensional step the diffusion and upwind advection operators are discretized implicitly by the Crank–Nicolson scheme [@cranknicolson1947], and the resulting tridiagonal systems are solved by the Thomas algorithm [@samarskii2001], vectorized over all grid lines. A second-order flux-limited antidiffusive correction with the van Leer limiter [@vanleer1974] is added explicitly to the upwind fluxes, which keeps the scheme essentially monotone at the high Péclet numbers found above. Boundary conditions (3) and (5) are discretized consistently with the Crank–Nicolson averaging, and the net fluxes through all boundaries are accumulated to evaluate budget (19) at every step. The Crank–Nicolson scheme is not unconditionally positivity-preserving, and the antidiffusive correction is applied explicitly from the previous time level; in addition, directional splitting applies the corrections sequentially. As a result, small negative undershoots can still appear, mainly at the leading edges of plumes where the emission switches on abruptly and the concentration gradients are steepest. They are set to zero, and the mass added by this operation is accounted for explicitly in the budget (Section 8.4). The particle velocities are updated at every step from system (2), and the meteorological forcing is updated hourly while the concentration field and the budget are carried over continuously. With constant forcing, a run split into one-hour segments reproduces an uninterrupted run bit for bit.
